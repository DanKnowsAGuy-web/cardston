The problem

The arena and the pool are Cardston's two biggest energy users.

In almost every small Alberta town, one building runs the compressors that make ice, and another heats water for swimming. Between them they carry more of the electricity and gas bill than every other municipal building combined.

Four things drive that cost at a facility like Charlie Cheesman Memorial Ice Arena and the Cardston Swimming Pool:

  • Ice plant refrigeration. The compressors that hold the ice sheet run nearly around the clock through the season and are typically the single largest electrical load in the building.
  • Pool water and air heating. Pool water, deck air, and humidity control are usually heated by natural gas or electric resistance, running for the length of the swim season.
  • Dehumidification. Ice and warm, moist pool air both fight condensation and air quality, and the equipment that manages it runs whenever the building is open.
  • Demand charges. Electricity bills for a facility like this are not just about kilowatt hours used. A large share of the bill is set by the single highest 15 minute peak in the month, and in many Alberta rate structures that peak keeps costing the town for months afterward through a demand ratchet, whether or not the equipment runs that hard again.

Cardston is one of only two towns in Alberta that own and operate their own municipal electrical distribution system, rather than being served by a wires company like FortisAlberta. Source and detail below.

The approach

Easy wins first. Nothing invented, everything measured.

We start with data the town already has, not with a sales estimate.

Phase 1: diagnostics

Twelve months of interval electricity data (with the town's authorization) plus equipment nameplates from the ice plant, pool mechanical room, and HVAC systems. That is all we need to start. No site visit is required to begin, though one usually follows once the data points somewhere specific.

From that diagnostic, the measures we expect to find room for, roughly in order of payback:

  • Ice plant and refrigeration optimization. Head pressure control, condenser and evaporator performance, compressor sequencing and controls tuning. Usually the fastest payback in the building.

  • Heat recovery from the ice plant to the pool and domestic hot water. The ice plant rejects heat it currently throws away outdoors; that heat can preheat pool water and hot water instead of burning gas or electricity to make it twice. This is a well proven retrofit at other Canadian arena and pool complexes; see the case notes below.

  • HVAC and controls. Scheduling, setpoints, and dehumidification tuning across the arena and pool mechanical systems.

  • Power factor correction and demand management. Shaving and shifting the peaks that set the demand charge, which matters most under a ratchet-style rate.

  • Solar thermal for pool heating, where the site suits it. Cardston's pool operates seasonally, in the warmer months, which is favourable for solar thermal compared with a year-round indoor pool. Alberta winters are a real limit on solar thermal generally, so we don't count on it before the diagnostic confirms roof orientation, shading, and the pool's actual heating load. Suitability is confirmed by the diagnostics, not assumed up front.

Phase 2: deeper measures

Once we're in and the first round of measures is running, Phase 2 looks at bigger equipment decisions, envelope work, and further electrification, with the town under no obligation to continue past Phase 1 if the numbers don't support it.

Financing

Paid for by the savings it creates. Nothing added to the capital plan.

Savings-funded, off the town's balance sheet

The project is financed against the energy savings it generates, not against Cardston's general revenue or capital budget. There is no upfront capital outlay from the town and no new debt on the town's books.

Pooled across several buildings

Bundling the arena, the pool, the town office, and water/wastewater into one project is what makes the numbers work at Cardston's scale, clearing minimum project sizes that any single small building usually can't reach alone.

Grants and rebates stacked and handled for the town

Where they're available — through programs like the Municipal Climate Change Action Centre (MCCAC), the Federation of Canadian Municipalities' Green Municipal Fund, or the Canada Infrastructure Bank's building retrofit programs — we identify, apply for, and stack them into the financing so the town doesn't have to run a grant application on its own.

Timing matters

Provincial recreation-facility retrofit grants have tightened over the last two years, with the MCCAC's dedicated recreation program now closed to new applications and its general conservation program closing its most recent intake in spring 2026. Financing the project from its own savings means Cardston isn't waiting on the next grant window to open.

What we need from Cardston

Low friction. Three things, none of them a budget ask.

  1. 01

    A signed data authorization

    A short form authorizing release of twelve months of interval electricity data from Cardston's own electrical utility records. This is the single most useful thing the town can provide, and it costs nothing.

  2. 02

    Recent utility bills

    Copies of the last twelve to twenty-four months of electricity and gas bills for the arena, pool, town office, and water/wastewater accounts.

  3. 03

    Equipment photos and nameplates, from a phone

    Photos of the ice plant compressors, pool heaters, and major HVAC nameplates. No special equipment needed, just a phone walking through each mechanical room.

Cardston's own electrical distribution utility, not FortisAlberta, will be the correct source for interval data specific to town-owned meters. We'll confirm the right request with the town directly once we're engaged; see research-notes.md.
